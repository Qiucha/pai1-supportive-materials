Where we are

Week 1 · Chance. Use a probability distribution to choose an outcome. Then see how evidence changes which outcomes count.

Why would a writing assistant ever choose its second-favorite word?

Sampling can choose a token with a smaller probability. Pick the highest always chooses the most likely token at each step.

A rule for the next token

A token is one unit of text. Our fictional assistant uses whole words as tokens. Its next-token probabilities add up to one.

Sample chooses a token at random with those probabilities. Pick the highest chooses the token with the largest probability at each step. We call this rule greedy decoding.

After the assistant appends a token, the full text so far is its updated prefix. That prefix determines the next distribution. A less likely first token can give the second token a larger chance.

Interactive 2

Count the faces that remain

Choose event E and the evidence, then compare the original fraction with the fraction among remaining faces.

The sample space S contains the six faces of a fair die. An event is a subset of those faces. Conditioning counts only faces that the evidence leaves possible.

F is fixed. Roll an even number, with faces {2, 4, 6}.

Each die sits in the sets it belongs to. The overlap is E ∩ F; everything inside either circle is E ∪ F. Everything outside E is its complement.

Crossed-out dice are ruled out by the evidence. Their set membership stays the same.

Original probabilities, before evidence

The inclusive union counts an overlapping face once.

Independence is a property of these events. In another model, you must state it as an assumption and justify it.

The math

Multiply along a sampled continuation

The second choice depends on the prefix. Multiply the first probability by the second probability given that first token.

$P(\text{warm glow})=P(\text{warm})P(\text{glow}\mid\text{warm})$
$0.50\times0.75=0.375$

Under Sample, warm glow has a 37.5% chance. Pick the highest always chooses warm glow in this example.

Count complements, unions, and evidence

The complement contains every face outside E. Its count is six minus the count in E.

$P(E^c)=1-P(E)$

Add the counts in E and F for their union. Subtract the intersection because that addition counts it twice.

$P(E\cup F)=P(E)+P(F)-P(E\cap F)$

For evidence F with positive probability, divide the probability of both events by the probability of F.

$P(E\mid F)=\frac{P(E\cap F)}{P(F)},\quad P(F)>0$

A six occupies one of six faces, but one of three even faces. Its chance changes from 1/6 to 1/3. A multiple of three occupies two of six faces and one of three even faces. Its chance stays at 1/3.

F together with its complement contains the whole sample space. Evidence that allows every face changes nothing.

$F\cup F^c=S,\qquad P(E\mid S)=P(E)$
Separate exclusivity from independence

Mutually exclusive events have no outcomes in common.

$E\cap F=\varnothing\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(E\cap F)=0$

Then $P(E\mid F)=0$ when $P(F)>0$. The reverse conditional $P(F\mid E)=0$ requires $P(E)>0$.

Independence means that the joint probability equals the product of the individual probabilities.

$P(E\cap F)=P(E)P(F)$

Odd and even are mutually exclusive and dependent. Knowing the roll is even rules out odd. Multiples of three and even overlap and are independent. One of the three even faces is a multiple of three, just as two of all six faces are.

Rolling a 1, 2, 3, or 4 is also independent of even. Its chance is $4/6=2/3$ overall and $2/3$ among even faces.

Reveal the lantern answer

Faint light has probability $0.30\times0.60=18\%$. Warm light has probability $0.50\times0.25=12.5\%$. Light's conditional probability depends on the first token.

2. What changes with even evidence?

The roll was even. Which chance changes, a six or a multiple of three? Make a prediction first.

Reveal the die answer

A six changes from 1/6 to 1/3. A multiple of three stays at 1/3. The first event depends on even evidence. The second event is independent of it.

3. What does the first flip tell you?

Two fresh flips of a fair coin are independent. The first was heads. Predict the chance of heads on the second.

Reveal the coin answer

The chance is 1/2. The stated independence makes the first flip irrelevant to the probability for the second flip.

Going deeper

What if the evidence has probability zero?

The elementary conditional probability ratio divides by $P(F)$. If $P(F)=0$, division by zero leaves this conditional undefined. The product definition of independence still applies when an event has probability zero or one.

When $0<P(F)<1$, both F and its complement have positive probability. Independence is then equivalent to the following equality.

$P(E)=P(E\mid F)=P(E\mid F^c)$
How do real text models differ?

Real tokens can be pieces of words. A model uses its full prefix at every step. Our fictional assistant has only two steps and a small table of probabilities.

A sampled continuation's probability is the product of its successive conditional probabilities. The full prefix conditions each factor.

$P(t_1,\ldots,t_n\mid x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}P(t_i\mid x,t_1,\ldots,t_{i-1})$

Here x is the starting prefix and each t is a token.

Return to Sample 20

Define success as warm followed by glow in one fresh run. Each run starts with the same prefix and sampling rules. Each run independently succeeds with probability 0.375.

Give a success the value 1 and any other continuation the value 0. This is a success indicator. How many successes do you expect in 20 runs? How much could the count vary?

The expected count is $20\times0.375=7.5$. That is an average across repeated batches. One batch has a whole-number count and need not match that average. Return to Sample and Run 20 to compare batches.
