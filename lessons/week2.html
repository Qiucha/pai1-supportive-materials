Where we are

Week 2 · Models. Week 1 introduced events and their probabilities. Week 2 uses those probabilities to model yes/no outcomes and counts.

If students report only how many statements they agree with, can we estimate how many used ChatGPT to cheat on an assignment or test?

Start with yes/no outcomes and counts on a fair die, then apply the same models to a two-list survey.

One outcome, then a count

A random variable assigns a number to an outcome. On one fair die roll, the face can be 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, or 6. Each face has probability $1/6$.

Ask "Did it land on 5?" Let $Y=1$ for yes and $Y=0$ for no. Then $P(Y=1)=1/6$ and $P(Y=0)=5/6$. This is a Bernoulli outcome.

Now ask "How many 5s did we get?" Let $X$ count the 5s in a batch of $n$ rolls. Each roll is one trial. A Binomial model counts successes in a fixed number of independent trials, each with the same success probability $p$. Here, $p=1/6$ and $n$ counts rolls in this batch.

Two lists, one reported total

A survey can count yeses too. Give each respondent one of two lists. Ask them to report how many statements they agree with. They do not report each answer.

Control list

  1. I am a member of the class management board.
  2. I have earned an A or A+ grade in a subject.
  3. I plan to take summer courses.
  4. I plan to pursue a master's degree.

Report one total: 0 through 4.

Treatment list

  1. I am a member of the class management board.
  2. I have earned an A or A+ grade in a subject.
  3. I plan to take summer courses.
  4. I plan to pursue a master's degree.
  5. I have used ChatGPT to cheat on assignments or tests.

Report one total: 0 through 5.

The treatment list adds one sensitive statement to the four shared statements. Comparing the groups' average totals can estimate how often that extra statement got a yes.

We adapted the list design from Nguyen and Goto, 2024, Unmasking academic cheating behavior in the artificial intelligence era: Evidence from Vietnamese undergraduates. These totals are simulated. Our model gives each shared item a 45% yes chance. It assumes the four shared answers are independent. It also assumes that the sensitive answer and the shared count are independent. These are teaching assumptions, not facts about the study respondents.

The math

One roll and a count

A Bernoulli variable has two values. For one roll, $Y\sim\operatorname{Ber}(p)$ with $p=1/6$.

$$P(Y=1)=p,\qquad P(Y=0)=1-p.$$

For a fixed $n$ independent rolls, $X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)$. One specified arrangement of $k$ successes has probability $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$. There are $\binom{n}{k}$ ways to place those successes among the rolls. Add their probabilities for $k = 0, \ldots, n$.

$$P(X=k)=\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$$

The factor $\binom{n}{k}=n!/[k!(n-k)!]$ counts those placements. Here $p=1/6$.

A count and a hidden rate

Let $H$ count yeses on the four shared items. Let $S$ be 1 for a sensitive yes and 0 for a sensitive no. The treatment total is $A=H+S$, and $p_s$ is the probability of a sensitive yes.

$$H\sim\operatorname{Bin}(4,0.45),\quad S\sim\operatorname{Ber}(p_s)$$

A total of $k$ has two possible causes. A sensitive no leaves $H=k$. A sensitive yes requires $H=k-1$. We assume $H$ and $S$ are independent, so the probabilities multiply in each case.

$$\begin{aligned}P(A=k)&=(1-p_s)P(H=k)\\&\quad+p_sP(H=k-1)\end{aligned}$$

Set $P(H=j)=0$ when $j$ is outside 0 through 4. The predicted mean is

$$E[A]=4(0.45)+p_s=1.80+p_s.$$

For the comparison between groups, assume their shared counts have equal expectations. Linearity of expectation then gives

$$E[A_{\mathrm{treatment}}]-E[H_{\mathrm{control}}]=p_s.$$

This mean argument does not require independence between $H$ and $S$. Subtract the control sample mean from the treatment sample mean to estimate $p_s$.

Predict, then reveal

1. Which model fits?

Classify each variable before you open the answer: the face on one die roll, whether it landed on 5, and the number of 5s in ten rolls.

Reveal the models

The face is a six-outcome random variable. Whether it landed on 5 is a Bernoulli outcome with $p=1/6$. The number of 5s in ten independent rolls is a Binomial count with $n=10$ and $p=1/6$.

2. What can one total reveal?

A treatment respondent reports 3. Can you determine their sensitive answer?

Reveal what 3 tells us

No. They could have three shared yeses and a sensitive no. They could also have two shared yeses and a sensitive yes.

What if they report 5? Predict before you open the answer.

Reveal what 5 tells us

All five answers must be yes. This total reveals the sensitive answer.

3. What can two groups reveal?

Use the simulated group means above to estimate the sensitive yes rate. What difference do you get?

Reveal the estimate and the study results

The simulation's generating rate is 23.7%. A finite sample estimate can differ from the rate that generated it.

Separately, Nguyen and Goto's real study estimated 23.7% through the list survey. When asked directly, 9.6% said yes. These published results describe the study population. Our simulated totals do not reproduce the study data or establish a rate for all students.

Going deeper

Comparable groups

Random assignment makes groups comparable on average. If the extra item changes answers to the shared items, those changes also affect the difference in totals.

Limits of privacy

A treatment total of 0 reveals that every answer is no. A treatment total of 5 reveals that every answer is yes. Both totals reveal the sensitive answer.

Sample variation

Another sample can give a different estimate. The published study's approximate 95% confidence interval was 10% to 37%. This interval describes uncertainty in the study estimate. We did not compute it from the simulated totals.
