The math

Crash counts

Let $X$ count vehicles involved in crashes meeting the injury criterion over the specified exposure. The hub publishes the mileage-blended expected count $\lambda=57.876$ for $E=271.3$ million miles. That corresponds to an implied benchmark rate of $r \approx 0.21333$ vehicles per million miles.

$$\lambda = r\,E \approx 0.21333 \times 271.3 \approx 57.876$$

A probability per trial, a rate per million miles, and an expected count over total exposure are different quantities.

$$X\sim\operatorname{Poi}(57.876)$$
$$\begin{aligned}P(X\le 3)&=\sum_{j=0}^{3}e^{-57.876}\frac{57.876^j}{j!}\\&\approx 2.49\times 10^{-21}\end{aligned}$$

Sum all counts at least as low as 3 under the benchmark model.

Count models

A Bernoulli variable records one yes/no trial with probability $p$. A Binomial variable counts successes in $n$ independent trials with the same probability $p$.

Our Poisson model assumes independent occurrences at a constant average rate over exposure. It models a count, rather than a fixed number of trials. For a rare-event Binomial approximation, use $\lambda=np$.

Miss rates and uncertainty
$$\hat p=\frac{\text{missed pedestrians}}{\text{labeled pedestrians}}$$
$$\text{gap (pp)}=100(\hat p_{\text{child}}-\hat p_{\text{adult}})$$

A rate measured on $n$ cases is uncertain by about $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$. Here $p$ is the rate, estimated from the sample, and $n$ is the number of cases. This approximates one standard error under independent sampling. It is not a guaranteed error bound.

Calibration

Within each confidence group, compare mean reported confidence with the observed fraction.

$$\text{observed}=\frac{\text{actual pedestrians}}{\text{proposed detections}}$$
$$\text{gap}=\text{confidence}-\text{observed}$$

Multiply the gap by 100 to express it in percentage points.

Going deeper

When Poisson approximates Binomial

Let $n$ grow while $p$ shrinks and $np$ stays fixed. Each trial rarely succeeds, but many trials together still produce a count. The Binomial distribution approaches a Poisson distribution with $\lambda=np$.

Reading very small probabilities

Decimal and scientific notation express the same quantity: $0.005=5\times10^{-3}$. A base-10 logarithm records the exponent: $\log_{10}(0.005)\approx-2.3010$.

The crash tail is about 0.00000000000000000000249. Its base-10 logarithm is about $-20.60$. Computers can represent this value. Much smaller values can underflow to zero because floating-point numbers have a finite range.

Designing a fair safety test

Match the injury definition, driving exposure, and driving conditions. City streets and highways can have different risks. A p-value answers the specified comparison under its assumptions. It cannot certify every aspect of safety.

What this unlocks

Does rain increase a pedestrian detector's miss rate? Compare labeled clips from rainy and dry drives with similar lighting and pedestrian distances. Test 100 labeled pedestrians per condition.

Before you see the illustrative counts, choose an increase that would trigger investigation. An increase equal to your threshold counts as a trigger.

Could this be chance?

The illustrative counts are 8 misses in rain and 3 in dry drives, out of 100 each. The rates are 8% and 3%. The increase is 5 percentage points.

Under the null comparison of equal miss rates, pool the samples: $\hat p=11/200=0.055$. For independent pedestrians, use the pooled standard error:

$$\sqrt{0.055\times0.945\times(1/100+1/100)}\approx0.0322$$

The two-proportion test gives $z\approx1.55$ and two-sided $p\approx0.12$.

This difference could easily be chance. A threshold alone is not enough to settle the safety claim. The p-value does not prove equal miss rates either.

Try another threshold. The same evidence can trigger different decisions. More independent observations would help estimate the difference.
